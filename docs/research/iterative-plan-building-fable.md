**Summary.** The operator's hypothesis, that the rules of effective software work have not changed, that humans and agents are fallible, and that early frequent delivery, spikes into unknowns and co-evolving plans, learning and code are what made iteration work, is mostly right about mechanisms and mostly unproven about outcomes. Iteration as a knowledge-buying discipline is documented from the 1950s, and every mechanism the 2025–2026 agent literature rediscovers appears in papers from 1970 to 1988. The outcome evidence for agile and continuous delivery is mixed and mostly observational, with causal attribution limited; the most rigorous results concern properties of individual changes (in the studied systems, the breadth of a change predicts failure at least as well as its size) and the fallibility of forecasts in the studied settings (optimistic, overconfident, fat-tailed, and not narrowing with time). What the agent evidence changes is the price list: producing code is cheap, while verifying it and keeping a shared theory of the system current are the scarce goods; an agent's assessment of its own unfinished work is unreliable without executed evidence, and where one agent writes both test and code their errors can correlate. The usable path from objective to increments that follows is a risk-directed, rolling-wave plan: state the objective with a signal and an investment limit; list unknowns and the decisions that are expensive to reverse; investigate only where the answer can change a consequential decision; inspect or establish the end-to-end path early; slice the rest into integrated increments that each carry grounded expected behaviour, discriminating checks and a declared reach; detail only the next increment; consolidate when evidence shows structure degrading; record plan changes with reasons; and put the operator's decisions at commitments and outcomes rather than at every diff. The joint recommendation reconciled with the host study refines this direction and is authoritative where the two differ.

## The hypothesis as four claims, and what the evidence does to each

The operator's hypothesis bundles four claims. Taking them apart is the first step, because the evidence treats them differently.

**Claim 1: the rules have not changed.** Supported as a statement about mechanisms, with a qualification. Every mechanism the agent literature now rediscovers is in the 1970s and 1980s papers: build to learn what cannot be analysed, keep a fallback, order by risk, hide decisions likely to change, consolidate as you grow, keep the running system as the oracle. But the historical rules were always conditional, and the conditions are stated by the same authors: explicit objectives and risk identification before each cycle, expertise to tell high-risk from low-risk work, and a few irreversible decisions taken early. What has changed is the economics those rules operate under, and that changes which rules bind. Code is cheap; verification, review and shared understanding are the scarce goods.

**Claim 2: humans and agents are fallible in the same way.** Supported, with a difference in kind. In the studied settings human forecasts are optimistic and overconfident and did not narrow with time; agents are optimistic about work they have not run, assess their own unfinished work unreliably without executed evidence, and act on under-specified instructions rather than ask. The difference is that a human team retains a theory of the system between sessions and an agent does not. For humans, iteration corrects the forecast; for agents, iteration corrects it only if the checks discriminate right from wrong on the actual deliverable and the theory is carried in artifacts.

**Claim 3: early frequent delivery and spikes into unknowns made iteration effective.** Partly supported and partly not. Delivery frequency by itself shows absence of harm in the industrial case studies, not measured benefit, and its benefits arrive bundled with automation, testing and flagging. The change-level studies support attending to the reach of each change as well as its size. The review studies support small changes because reviewers understand them, not because they find more defects. Spikes are well defined by their inventors and have no outcome evidence; the prototyping case literature shows that throwaway code is usually kept, and that keeping it is where maintainability goes. The strongest support for early delivery is indirect: most ideas fail when tested, forecasts in the studied settings have fat tails, and integration surprises drive the tail, so the earliest real end-to-end slice is a cheap place to find out.

**Claim 4: plans, learning and code evolving together made iteration effective.** Supported as a practice that the successful cases share (Basili and Turner's project control list, Parnas and Clements' documents that record what is unknown, decision records, progress notes in agent harnesses) and, at the same time, named by the 2026 practitioner literature as the thing most at risk when agents write faster than people can understand. The historical evidence does not show that co-evolution caused success; it shows that the projects described as successful kept their plan current and that the ones described as failures let code outrun architecture and understanding.

The rest of the study gives the evidence behind each verdict, then draws the mechanisms out so that the boundaries follow from them, and ends with a usable path from objective to increments.

## The historical record: iteration is old, conditional and thinly measured

Larman and Basili's chronology places iterative and incremental development in the 1950s: Project Mercury ran half-day, time-boxed iterations with test-first development, and large 1970s systems (IBM's Trident submarine command system, the LAMPS avionics project, NASA's Shuttle flight software with 17 iterations over 31 months) were built in increments while DoD standards nominally required a single pass. The authors state that they chose a chronology rather than a comparative analysis, that the list is representative rather than complete, and that the methods varied in iteration length and time-boxing. Several flagship successes used six-month iterations after a significant up-front specification. The success claims are the participants' own, some reach the paper as personal communications, and no failed iterative project is listed. [Larman and Basili 2003, pp. 47–54](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~basili/publications/journals/J90.pdf)

Royce's 1970 paper, usually cited as the origin of the waterfall, calls the single-pass version of its own diagram risky, because timing, storage and input-output behaviour can only be experienced rather than analysed, and prescribes a "do it twice" pilot of about a third of the schedule on the ground that judgment in such matters is invariably optimistic. It also prescribes strict documentation. Royce's iteration is a developer-side rehearsal, not customer-facing incremental delivery, and Larman and Basili concede it is not classic iterative development. [Royce 1970, pp. 329–335 of the ICSE-9 reprint](https://web.archive.org/web/2019id_/http://www-scf.usc.edu/~csci201/lectures/Lecture11/royce1970.pdf)

Boehm's spiral model is the first explicit statement that the order of work should be set by risk: each cycle identifies objectives, alternatives and constraints, locates the uncertainties that are significant sources of risk, and resolves them by prototyping, simulation, benchmarking, reference checking, user questionnaires or analysis before committing. The same paper carries the strongest early warnings about iteration. Boehm writes that evolutionary development is hard to distinguish from the code-and-fix model the waterfall was invented to cure, and that such projects come to grief by evolving hard-to-change code before settling architecture and usage, especially when independently evolved pieces must later be integrated, when work-arounds harden into constraints, or when a large existing system is being replaced. For compilers or secure operating systems he still prefers fully elaborated documents. The spiral depends on developers' ability to identify and manage risk, and he warns that a team which elaborates the easy parts first gives "an illusion of progress" while heading for disaster. The paper's quantitative support is one organisation's productivity compared with cost-model predictions. [Boehm 1988, pp. 61–70](https://faculty.washington.edu/hazeline/misc/reserve/boehm_spiralmodel.pdf)

Basili and Turner's 1975 iterative enhancement is the earliest method paper in this corpus and already contains the elements later practice rediscovered: start with a skeletal subproject, keep a project control list that measures the distance to the final implementation, analyse each increment with the rule that modifications should become easier as iterations progress (difficulty signals a design flaw or a proliferation of patches), and solicit user reaction on each increment. The evidence is a single compiler project the authors describe as preliminary. [Basili and Turner 1975, pp. 390–395](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~basili/publications/journals/J04.pdf)

Gilb's evolutionary delivery adds two demands the later agile literature often dropped: each step must have a quantified, measurable objective, and a retreat path to the previous successful step. His own 2002 teaching handout reports Hewlett-Packard research hypotheses that early feedback raises schedule-estimation error and that early market feedback raises bugginess, so the practice's inventor publishes its costs alongside its benefits. [Gilb 2002, pp. 2–10](https://static.aminer.org/pdf/PDF/000/306/364/evolutionary_project_management_multiple_performance_quality_and_cost_metrics_for.pdf)

Four essays supply the reasoning that connects these practices. Parnas and Clements list seven reasons why a rational, top-down process is impossible: commissioners do not know exactly what they want, details emerge only during implementation, people cannot hold all the details, requirements change for external reasons, and humans err; they nevertheless argue for documents that look rational, with missing information marked where it belongs, because finding facts before coding helps the team "design better and backtrack less". [Parnas and Clements 1986, §§II–VII](https://users.ece.utexas.edu/~perry/education/SE-Intro/fakeit.pdf) Brooks's No Silver Bullet argues that clients cannot specify completely before having built and tried some versions, and that software should be grown by incremental development rather than built; he calls it the change that most improved his own practice. [Brooks 1986, §§5.2–5.3](https://www.cs.unc.edu/techreports/86-020.pdf) Armour's orders of ignorance explain why building is the default process for discovering what one does not know one does not know: attempting to build the system is always available as a way to make the customer reveal what was missing. [Armour 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/2019id_/https://cacm.acm.org/magazines/2000/10/7556-the-five-orders-of-ignorance/fulltext) Naur's theory-building view holds that the knowledge produced by programming lives in the programmers, that reviving a program from documentation alone is "strictly impossible", and that no methodologically satisfactory study of programming methods' efficacy had been made. [Naur 1985](https://pablo.rauzy.name/dev/naur1985programming.pdf)

Shewhart and Deming's plan-do-study-act cycle, which Larman and Basili name as the ancestor of time-boxed iteration, carries the epistemic structure the software authors borrowed: predict, test on a small scale, study the result against the prediction, act. [Moen and Norman 2010, pp. 25–27](https://deming.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/circling-back.pdf)

**What this record establishes and what it does not.** It establishes that iteration predates agile, was used on large high-stakes systems, and rests on a coherent account of why foreknowledge is limited. It does not establish that short iterations are necessary, that iteration beats staged development in general, or that failure statistics attributed to the waterfall are sound; Saravanos's 2025 history notes that the Standish figures were questioned because executives were asked to share failure stories. [Saravanos 2025, pp. 1–3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03894) The record's own authors mark the boundaries: iteration needs explicit objectives and risk identification before each cycle, or it becomes code-and-fix; it needs expertise to tell high-risk from low-risk work; and the knowledge it produces is lost when the team that holds it dissolves.

## What the agile and XP literature measured, and what it only asserted

The one quality-graded systematic review of agile research covers studies to 2005. Of 1,996 citations, 36 were empirical; three quarters concerned XP and one concerned Scrum; 25 of the 33 primary studies lacked an appropriate recruitment strategy; and the authors grade the strength of evidence on agile's benefits and limitations "very low". Comparative productivity results ranged from +337%, which they trace to an XP team producing three and a half times the lines of code without more functionality, to −44%, and one study found no quality difference. They also note that the evidence did not make agile methods the obvious choice for large projects. [Dybå and Dingsøyr 2008, pp. 833–852](https://www.ic.unicamp.br/~wainer/outros/systrev/5.pdf)

The practices with the best outcome evidence for planning are frequent delivery to production and flexible scope, and that evidence is correlational and small. In 35 Norwegian public-sector projects assessed through 107 interviews, projects with one delivery into production had a 64% success rate, projects with up to four deliveries 77%, and projects with more than four 100% (eight projects); agile projects with flexible scope succeeded 87% of the time against 58% with fixed scope; time-and-materials contracts succeeded 83% against 38% for fixed price. The authors read agile success as depending on flexible scope and frequent delivery into production; they report no significance tests, the organisations were among the more competent, and the direction of causation is not addressed. [Mohagheghi and Jørgensen 2017, pp. 753–756](https://www.jsoftware.us/vol12/287-SE019.pdf) Jørgensen's 101-project questionnaire found no non-agile medium or large project rated acceptable (nine such projects) and turned the question around to how well non-agile methods work at scale, while calling the results exploratory and naming the field's confirmation bias: what works is called agile and what fails is called not truly agile. [Jørgensen 2018, pp. 181–189](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-319-91602-6_12.pdf)

Two famous numbers do not survive inspection. The claim that 45% of features are never used comes from a 2002 conference keynote based on four internal applications, with no published method. [Fowler 2002](https://martinfowler.com/articles/xp2002.html); [Cohn 2016](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/blog/are-64-of-features-really-rarely-or-never-used) The Standish success rates, including the 2015 table giving agile 39% and waterfall 11%, rest on definitions that measure estimation deviation only, are one-sided, and produce what Eveleens and Verhoef call "meaningless figures"; the 2015 table states no method. [Eveleens and Verhoef 2010, pp. 30–36](https://www.cs.vu.nl/~x/the_rise_and_fall_of_the_chaos_report_figures.pdf); [Standish 2015, p. 7](https://cdn1-public.infotech.com/agile/CHAOSReport2015-Final.pdf)

The XP sources themselves are careful. Beck's 1999 article presents the flattened cost-of-change curve as a conditional premise rather than a measurement, treats the period before first production as a dangerous anomaly, targets small to medium systems with vague and changing requirements, and says the limits of XP's application are not clear. [Beck 1999, pp. 70–77](https://www.cs.kent.edu/~jmaletic/cs63902/Papers/Beck99.pdf) The C3 payroll project that gave XP its name was cancelled; Fowler records that it shows XP is no guarantee of success, and Beck's own diagnosis was that the people paying for the system and the people evaluating it disagreed, "XP or no XP". [Fowler 2004](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/C3.html); [c2 wiki, CthreeProjectTerminated](http://c2.com/wiki/remodel/pages/CthreeProjectTerminated) Boehm and Turner's five factors (size, criticality, dynamism, personnel, culture) and their home-grounds table say the same thing as a framework: agile methods were untested on safety-critical products, rely on tacit knowledge that limits scale, and assume refactoring is cheap, which becomes expensive rework in highly stable environments. [Boehm and Turner 2003, ch. 2](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780321186126/samplepages/0321186125.pdf)

Two practice-level results matter for how increments should be shaped. In four industrial teams, test-driven development was associated with 40–90% lower pre-release defect density at a subjectively estimated 15–35% cost in initial development time, with non-equivalent comparison teams and no statistical tests. [Nagappan et al. 2008](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2009/10/Realizing-Quality-Improvement-Through-Test-Driven-Development-Results-and-Experiences-of-Four-Industrial-Teams-nagappan_tdd.pdf) When 39 professionals' processes were dissected, the order of test and code had no important influence; what predicted quality was granularity and uniformity of steps, which the authors read as TDD-like processes encouraging "fine-grained, steady steps". [Fucci et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.05994) Under Firefox's move to rapid releases, test executions per day more than doubled while the volunteer tester community fell from 6,010 unique testers to 105 and test scope narrowed. [Mäntylä et al. 2013](https://mmantyla.github.io/2013_Mantyla_On_Rapid_Releases_and_Software_Testing_Firefox_ICSM.pdf) Meyer's critique of agile supplies the boundary from the other side: user stories are not a sufficient basis for requirements, and refactoring cannot turn a bad design into a good one. [Meyer 2014](https://se.inf.ethz.ch/~meyer/publications/methodology/agile_software.pdf)

**Inference.** The agile evidence supports two planning rules and warns against a third. Deliver to real use early and often, and keep scope adjustable, because that is what the successful projects in the correlational data did. Make steps small and uniform, because that is the part of TDD the dissection study could attribute an effect to. Do not expect a method to compensate for misaligned goals or missing competence, because the field's own best-known failure and its best-known success factors both point there.

## Small batches and fast feedback: strong on mechanism, mixed on measured outcome

The practitioner case for small increments rests on queueing and feedback arguments. Reinertsen states them as principles (reducing batch size reduces cycle time, variability and risk, and accelerates feedback), explicitly as theory derived from queueing and manufacturing, with the reminder that economic batch size is a U-curve optimisation rather than a race to zero. [Reinertsen 2009, principles B1–B22, author's list](http://lpd2.com/sample-page/the-principles-of-flow/) Humble's statement of continuous delivery gives the same mechanism in practical form: large batches arise from the fixed cost of handing off changes, so the point of the pipeline is to change the economics until small batches are cheap. [Humble, Principles](https://continuousdelivery.com/principles/)

The measured evidence sorts into three kinds.

**Change-level risk studies are the most rigorous and the most specific.** At Bell Labs, failure probability of a change rose with the number of subsystems and modules touched and with lines added, fell with developer experience, and was higher for fixes than for new features; the authors single out change diffusion and developer experience as essential predictors. [Mockus and Weiss 2000](https://mockus.org/papers/bltj13.pdf) At Microsoft, absolute churn measures were poor predictors of defect density while relative churn, the proportion of a component changed, was highly predictive. [Nagappan and Ball 2005](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/icse05churn.pdf) Across eleven projects, the number of files touched, relative churn and fix-versus-feature were the most important risk-increasing factors. [Kamei et al. 2013](https://posl.ait.kyushu-u.ac.jp/~kamei/publications/Kamei_TSE2013.pdf) The practical reading, within the studied systems, is that the breadth of a change predicts failure at least as well as its line count. A plan that bounds increments should therefore consider their reach, not only their size.

**Industrial case studies of deployment frequency mostly report absence of harm.** At Facebook and OANDA over seven and five years, the number of critical issues stayed almost constant as deployments rose, low-severity issues scaled with lines deployed, a productivity surge in 2009–2010 coincided with more hot-fixes, and the authors stress that two companies are two data points and that they can only hypothesise causes. [Savor et al. 2016](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Savor-ICSE16.pdf) Facebook's mobile teams found that deployment frequency did not directly affect productivity or quality, with lower quality for deadline-day pushes and for files touched by many developers. [Rossi et al. 2016](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Rossi-FSE-16.pdf) Firefox's move to six-week releases produced no significant increase in post-release bugs and faster fixes, but users hit bugs earlier and a smaller proportion of bugs was fixed. [Khomh et al. 2012](https://swat.polymtl.ca/~foutsekh/docs/Khomh-MSR-2012.pdf) None of these isolates batch size from the automation, testing and feature-flag practices adopted with it.

**Survey evidence is broad and causally weak.** DORA's reports, from 2014 through 2025, use a cross-sectional, self-reported design with organic recruitment (which the reports themselves describe as snowball sampling) supplemented in recent years by a panel channel, analysed with correlation-based structural models; the 2024 report says plainly that it ran neither longitudinal studies nor an experiment and that temporal precedence is theoretical. [DORA 2024, p. 110](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/2024-dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-report.pdf) Keunwoo Lee's review of Accelerate adds the circularity objection: defining high performance by deployment frequency and then reporting that deployment practices predict it. [Lee 2022](https://keunwoo.com/notes/accelerate-devops/) The reports remain useful as a map of what co-occurs; they do not establish that adopting small batches causes the outcomes.

**Code review converges on small changes for reasons other than defect finding.** Review practice at Google, Microsoft, AMD and large open-source projects has converged on small changes with one or two reviewers and hour-scale turnaround; the Google study reports a median of 24 lines changed, 35% of changes touching one file, and median initial feedback under an hour for small changes against about five hours for very large ones. [Rigby and Bird 2013](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~pcr/paper/Rigby2013FSE.pdf); [Sadowski et al. 2018](https://sback.it/publications/icse2018seip.pdf) Only about one-eighth to one-seventh of review comments concern defects; the rest are understanding, maintainability and knowledge transfer. [Bacchelli and Bird 2013](https://sback.it/publications/icse2013.pdf); [Czerwonka et al. 2015](https://www.michaelagreiler.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Code-Reviews-Do-Not-Find-Bugs.-How-the-Current-Code-Review-Best-Practice-Slows-Us-Down.pdf) The widely repeated 200–400 line rule comes from one vendor-run case study whose author notes in a footnote that the result assumes true defect density is constant across change sizes. [Cohen 2006, footnote 5](https://static1.smartbear.co/support/media/resources/cc/book/code-review-cisco-case-study.pdf) Google's own guidance is a heuristic with the same shape: about 200 lines, with the warning that a change spread across many files is too large at a far lower count. [Software Engineering at Google, ch. 9](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch09.html)

**Inference.** Small increments are justified first by what they do for the people and tools that must understand them: a reviewer, a test suite, a bisect, a rollback, or an agent with a finite context all work better on a change whose reach is narrow and whose purpose is single. The defect-probability evidence supports attending to reach; the review evidence supports bounding what a reader must hold in mind; the case studies say frequency itself is neither a cure nor a hazard. Review capacity, not defect finding, is the binding constraint, and no opened study measures what happens to review quality when the volume of small changes rises sharply. That gap matters most when an agent is the author, and the agent evidence below returns to it.

## In the studied settings forecasts err toward optimism, so order work by risk and buy knowledge where it is cheapest

**The forecast is the weakest link, and in the studied settings the error runs toward optimism.** Kahneman and Tversky named the planning fallacy in 1977 and attributed it to an inside view that neglects distributional data. [Kahneman and Tversky 1977, p. 2-2](https://archive.org/download/DTIC_ADA047747/DTIC_ADA047747.pdf) Students who predicted 33.9 days to finish a thesis took 55.5, and only 10.8% finished by their best-case date; the bias disappeared when predictors were made to connect the forecast to their own past experience, so it is removable. [Buehler, Griffin and Ross 1994, pp. 366–369](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/67_J_Personality_and_Social_Psychology_366,_1994.pdf) Software professionals' 90% confidence intervals for effort contained the actual effort in 35% of cases, and interval width did not respond to the confidence level requested. [Jørgensen, chapter on interval width](https://cms.simula.no/sites/default/files/publications/Simula.simula.848.pdf) Across 106 projects at one company, the ratio of actual to estimated duration was lognormal with a p10–p90 spread of about three to four, and relative remaining uncertainty stayed roughly constant over the project's life, contrary to the textbook cone that narrows with time. [Little 2006, pp. 48–53](https://toddlittleweb.com/Papers/Little%20Cone%20of%20Uncertainty.pdf) In a sample of 1,471 IT projects the average overrun was 27%, but one in six was a Black Swan with a 200% average overrun; a later sample of 5,392 projects follows a power law whose proposed generator is interdependence among components, and the authors note that their data cannot distinguish agile from waterfall projects. [Flyvbjerg and Budzier 2011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1304.0265); [Flyvbjerg et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.01573)

**Most ideas fail, so delivery to real use is the discovery mechanism.** At Microsoft, only about one third of well-designed experiments improved the metric they were meant to improve; Bing's rate was 10–20%; Google reported roughly 10% of about 12,000 experiments leading to business changes. [Kohavi et al. 2009, p. 5](https://www.exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf); [Kohavi et al. 2013, p. 2](https://www.exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf); [Kohavi et al. 2012, p. 1](https://www.exp-platform.com/Documents/puzzlingOutcomesInControlledExperiments.pdf) These figures cover ideas that reached testing at a few large online firms, and the same authors document how experiments themselves mislead through novelty effects, non-replicating surprises and low prior rates that make a borderline p-value weak evidence. The product-discovery literature that builds on this point (Cagan's dual-track discovery, Torres's opportunity trees, Patton's story maps, Adzic's impact maps, Basecamp's Shape Up) gives prescriptions for turning an objective into testable bets, and none of it offers outcome evidence beyond anecdote; Shape Up states that it does not address the risk of building the wrong thing. [Singer 2019, ch. 1](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/0.3-chapter-01)

**One common justification for early iteration does not survive the evidence.** The claim that late defects cost ten to a hundred times more to fix was tested on 171 projects and 47,376 defect logs, and no delayed-issue effect was found. [Menzies et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.04886) The surviving case for early spikes and increments rests on value discovery, risk exposure and the fat tail of integration surprises, not on defect economics.

**Risk-driven ordering is the practitioners' answer.** Boehm's spiral set the pattern; Fairbanks reduces it to a loop usable at any scale: identify and prioritise risks, apply techniques, evaluate the reduction, with effort proportional to the risk of failure and the caveat that the model cannot make the judgment calls. [Fairbanks 2010, ch. 1](https://www.georgefairbanks.com/assets/jesa/Introduction.pdf); [Fairbanks 2010, Methods & Tools excerpt](https://www.methodsandtools.com/archive/agilesoftwarearchitecture.php) Shape Up's version is to push the scariest work uphill first and to fix the appetite before the design, reversing the usual order in which estimates follow a design. [Singer 2019, chs. 3 and 12](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03) Kurtz and Snowden's framework explains why probing precedes planning in complex settings: emergent patterns can be perceived but not predicted, so the decision model is probe, sense, respond. [Kurtz and Snowden 2003, pp. 467–468](http://bitsavers.informatik.uni-stuttgart.de/pdf/ibm/IBM_Systems_Journal/423/kurtz.pdf)

**Spikes, skeletons and slices are distinct tools with distinct disposal rules.** On the original XP wiki Beck's rule is that spikes suit a team that is "knowledge-limited, not time-limited"; Jeffries adds a time-box of at most a couple of days, the intent to throw the code away, and the lesson that spikes which skipped the full breadth got the team into trouble. [c2 wiki, SpikeSolution](http://xp.c2.com/SpikeSolution.html) Cockburn's walking skeleton is the opposite kind of artifact: a tiny end-to-end implementation linking the main components, kept as production code with tests, whereas a spike demonstrates plausible technical success and is discarded. [Cockburn, Walking skeleton](https://web.archive.org/web/2016id_/http://alistair.cockburn.us/Walking+skeleton) Patton builds the skeleton first because the backbone of a story map describes the smallest end-to-end system. [Patton 2008](https://www.jpattonassociates.com/the-new-backlog/) Vertical slicing through the layers, Wake's INVEST criteria and Lawrence's splitting patterns turn the rest of the objective into increments; Lawrence places the spike last among nine patterns because a team usually knows enough to build something and learns more by doing so. [Wake 2003](https://xp123.com/articles/invest-in-good-stories-and-smart-tasks/); [Lawrence and Green](https://www.humanizingwork.com/the-humanizing-work-guide-to-splitting-user-stories/) Hypothesis-driven development gives each increment a falsifiable form: the capability, the outcome expected, and the measurable signal that would show it. [O'Reilly 2014](https://www.thoughtworks.com/insights/articles/how-implement-hypothesis-driven-development)

**Failure modes are documented by the practices' own authors.** In 34 prototyping case studies, eighteen recommended keeping the prototype and only seven insisted on discarding it, and every reported loss of maintainability came from kept prototypes: throwaway code is rarely thrown away, and keeping it is where quality goes. [Gordon and Bieman 1993](https://www.cs.colostate.edu/~bieman/Pubs/GordonBieman93SQJ.pdf) The Elephant Carpaccio exercise, which teaches slicing to minutes, ends with a debrief in which most participants rate their code quality one or two out of five, named as a pitfall of short iterations and small stories. [Kniberg and Cockburn 2013, p. 5](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/5e3bed81529ab12a517031ab/5ece238ef1473a6416860f46_Elephant_Carpaccio_exercise.pdf) Flyvbjerg, who argues for speed through modular replication, calls fast-tracking, which starts delivery before the design is settled, "common but mistaken", and his book leads with planning slowly and acting fast: cheap iteration belongs in planning through models and probes, and delivery should be short because forecast error grows with duration. [Flyvbjerg 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.11104)

**Inference.** Three things follow for plan building. First, the plan's own estimates should be treated as a distribution with a wide right tail, which argues for capping each bet by investment and scope rather than estimating it precisely. Second, the order of work should weigh what would hurt most if wrong against value and learning cost, and the end-to-end path should be inspected or established early because integration is where the fat tail lives. Third, each spike needs a question, a bound on investment and a disposal rule written down before it starts, because the pressure to keep it is documented and strong.

## What cannot be cheaply iterated, and how practitioners decide how much to design first

The evolutionary-design literature is explicit that iteration is safe only under conditions. Fowler's essay says that evolutionary design as commonly practised is a disaster, that XP's fundamental assumption is that tests, continuous integration and refactoring can flatten the change curve enough to make it work, that YAGNI becomes good practice only once the curve is flat, that a lack of will to design is a major reason evolutionary design fails, and that there is still "very little data". [Fowler 2004](https://martinfowler.com/articles/designDead.html) His definition of architecture as the things people perceive as hard to change, with the architect's task being to eliminate irreversibility, comes with Ralph Johnson's counterweight that making everything easy to change makes the whole system complex. [Fowler 2003, pp. 3–4](https://martinfowler.com/ieeeSoftware/whoNeedsArchitect.pdf) Reversibility is therefore a budget, not a goal.

Parnas supplies the mechanism and the precondition. Information hiding makes change cheap by hiding each decision likely to change behind an interface; it requires listing those decisions in advance. [Parnas 1972, pp. 1055–1058](https://www.win.tue.nl/~wstomv/edu/2ip30/references/criteria_for_modularization.pdf) His 1979 paper is the earliest explicit method for incremental delivery: search first for the minimal subset that could perform a useful service and then for a set of minimal increments, structure the "uses" relation so that useful subsets exist, and accept that flexibility cannot be an afterthought. [Parnas 1979, pp. 128–136](https://www.cs.drexel.edu/~yc349/CS575/Week2/Extension%20and%20Contraction.pdf) The walking skeleton and tracer bullet of later practice are this idea with a shorter name.

Growth without consolidation is the best-attested failure mode. Foote and Yoder's pattern catalogue treats architecture as a hypothesis about the future, warns that the real problem with throwaway code is that it is not thrown away, observes that piecemeal growth favours local concerns over architectural ones, and calls for "a permanent commitment to consolidation". [Foote and Yoder 1999](http://www.laputan.org/mud/mud.html) Cunningham's original debt metaphor was coined in exactly this setting, an incrementally grown product in which first-time code is a debt that is safe only while it is repaid promptly, and where the more serious pitfall is the failure to consolidate. [Cunningham 1992](http://c2.com/doc/oopsla92.html) The empirical anchors are a survey of 1,831 practitioners in three large organisations in which bad architecture choices were the top-ranked source of technical debt (54% of top-three responses) and were typically made years earlier, and a mining study of 466 versions of ten open-source systems in which architectural smells predicted issue- and change-proneness. [Ernst et al. 2015](https://insights.sei.cmu.edu/documents/1461/2015_021_001_453259.pdf); [Le et al. 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10293485)

Some properties cannot be added later at all. Leveson's position is that emergent properties such as safety cannot be added on or measured in afterwards; her remedy, safety-guided design, is itself iterative, with hazard analysis informing each design decision as it is made, so the claim is against retrofit rather than against iteration. [Leveson 2011, pp. 172–176, 251–252](https://archive.org/download/mit_press_book_9780262298247/mit_press_book_9780262298247.pdf) Kruchten names the project that illustrates the risk: fifty developers who "hit a wall" after six months of productive XP and Scrum because they believed a solid architecture would emerge from bi-weekly refactorings; he also argues that most projects' key architectural decisions are made in the first days by platform choice, so genuinely architectural work is needed only when the project is novel, large or critical. [Kruchten 2010, §§2–3](https://philippe.kruchten.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/09/kruchten-2010-contextualizingagilityfinal.pdf) Poppendieck's lean text, which argues for deciding as late as possible, still names a few basic architectural decisions that must be right at the beginning because they fix the system's constraints for its life, such as language, layering and sharing a database with other applications, and recommends deciding those few breadth-first with the best information available. [Poppendieck and Poppendieck 2003, ch. 3](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780321150783/samplepages/0321150783.pdf)

The one empirical study of how teams actually set the amount of up-front design is Waterman, Noble and Allan's grounded theory from 44 practitioners. Up-front effort tracks technical risk (challenging quality requirements, many integration points, legacy) and complexity rather than size. Requirements instability, contrary to earlier factor lists, does not raise up-front effort. Big design up-front, where it occurs, is driven mainly by customers who demand fixed-scope plans rather than by technical risk. Emergent architecture still fixes the technology stack and top-level style first, and doing no up-front design raises total effort because the architecture must then be evolved every iteration, a cost start-ups accept because it arrives later. Frameworks and template architectures convert former architectural decisions into ordinary design decisions. The rule they extract is that the higher a risk's impact, the earlier it should be mitigated. [Waterman, Noble and Allan 2015, §§IV–VI](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?repid=rep1&type=pdf&doi=a6ea3073800f62c81415095d36b0b49b81a5f822)

Decision records are the memory this process needs. Nygard's format exists because decisions are not all made at once, because the motivation behind them is among the hardest things to track, and because lost rationale produces either blind acceptance, in which a team becomes afraid to change anything, or blind reversal. [Nygard 2011](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions) Kruchten and colleagues add the proportionality rule: capture the decisions that matter, not every micro-decision, to avoid what they call knowledge vaporisation. [Kruchten, Capilla and Dueñas 2009, pp. 36–41](https://burjcdigital.urjc.es/server/api/core/bitstreams/4b5f23a1-f42e-4ff3-a608-c1707ead088b/content)

**Inference.** Iteration does not remove the need to decide; it changes when each decision is due. A plan that intends to iterate must therefore do three things before the first increment: identify the decisions that are expensive to reverse and decide those few early, with their rationale recorded; make the others cheap to reverse by hiding them behind interfaces; and treat consolidation as explicit work when evidence shows structure degrading rather than hoping it emerges. Everything else can and should wait for evidence.

## What the agent evidence adds: the rules hold, the prices have moved

**Productivity effects are measured for short, well-specified, greenfield work; for the current paired-agent workflow on brownfield work no reliable controlled estimate was located.** The three vendor-authored controlled studies report a 55.8% faster completion of a toy HTTP-server task with Copilot (35 completers per arm) [Peng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.06590), a 26.08% increase (standard error 10.3%) in completed tasks across three field experiments with 4,867 developers, stronger for short-tenure developers and with noisy, mixed quality effects [Cui et al. 2025](https://www.mertdemirer.com/Papers/Demirer_AI_productivity.pdf), and about 21% faster completion of a three-hour enterprise task at Google, with a confidence interval that crosses zero once covariates are included [Paradis et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.12944). METR's 2025 randomised study of 16 experienced maintainers on their own mature repositories, using early-2025 tools, found that allowing AI increased completion time by 19% while developers believed they were 20% faster, and the authors said the results were consistent with substantial speedups on small greenfield projects or in unfamiliar codebases; that study stands as mature-repository evidence for its tools and setting. [Becker et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089) In February 2026 METR judged its follow-up data an unreliable signal: returning developers showed a raw −18% (confidence interval −38% to +9%), new recruits −4% (−15% to +9%), from 57 developers and 800+ tasks, with the design broken by developers refusing to work without AI, 30–50% withholding tasks, and agentic parallel work making task timing meaningless. [METR 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) No reliable controlled estimate for the current paired-agent workflow on realistic brownfield work was located in the sources reviewed; METR 2025 remains valid evidence for its tools and setting. Perception is not a substitute: self-assessment correlated with measured output at r = 0.17 in the Stanford telemetry group's slides, whose matrix, labelled orientative, puts gains at +35–40% for simple greenfield work and +0–10% for complex brownfield work. [Denisov-Blanch 2025, slides](https://aiconference.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Yegor-Denisov-Blanch-Will-AI-Replace-Software-Engineers_-.pptx.pdf)

**Where the throughput goes.** Vendor telemetry, all correlational, agrees on a pattern: developers using AI completed 21% more tasks and merged 98% more pull requests, review time rose by 91%, pull requests grew 154% in size, bugs per developer rose 9%, and company-level delivery metrics stayed flat. [Faros AI 2025](https://243608892.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na2.net/hubfs/243608892/AI_Engineering_Impact_Report_July_2025_Faros_AI.pdf) An independent longitudinal study of an enterprise "2×" mandate across 802 developers and 196,212 pull requests found throughput rose about 12% per developer at adoption and kept growing, while review load per reviewer doubled, automated review overtook human review, and revert and post-merge fix rates stayed flat. [He et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.01904) DORA's survey work moved from a negative association in 2024 (−1.5% throughput, −7.2% stability per 25% increase in AI adoption) to a positive throughput association with instability still rising in 2025; the 2024 explanation, that changelists are growing in size, is offered as a hypothesis. [DORA 2024, pp. 39–40](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/2024-dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-report.pdf); [DORA 2025, p. 4](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf) DORA's 2025 capabilities model then reports that working in small batches, measured by lines per change, changes per release and task duration, amplifies AI's effect on product performance and turns its neutral effect on friction into a reduction, while slightly reducing perceived individual effectiveness gains; the authors describe the discipline as shifting focus from raw generation to decomposition, prompting and verification. [DORA AI Capabilities Model 2025, pp. 45–47](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_dora_ai_capabilities_model.pdf) Secondary reports say DORA did not run a survey in 2026; this is unconfirmed by a primary announcement, and DORA's research index listed 2025 as the newest annual report at the time of writing. [Waydev, 4 September 2026](https://waydev.co/dora-pauses-the-annual-survey/)

**More specification artifacts are not more quality.** A mining study of 88,052 pull requests in 119 repositories found that specification artifacts in pull-request workflows made no difference to defect introduction after matching (−0.6 percentage points, p = 0.133) and concluded that they proxy for task complexity; it does not test full up-front specification against increment-level planning. [Hill 2026](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol394-esem2026/LIPIcs.ESEM.2026.84/LIPIcs.ESEM.2026.84.pdf) Practitioners interviewed about spec-driven tools reported that spec runs consumed two to three times the tokens and about twice the time, that specs went stale as code changed, and that evaluation effort outgrew human ability to review. [Agarwal et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.25202) Agent performance fell by 27 points on average as structural task constraints accumulated in a backend benchmark, a result about constraint density rather than about planning. [Dente et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06445) Böckeler's hands-on review of three spec-driven tools called the ceremony for a small bug "a sledgehammer to crack a nut" and concluded that small iterative steps are the better way to stay in control. [Böckeler 2025](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html) Together these support treating full detail as a risk-and-maintenance choice with exceptions, not a causal conclusion that complete specification cannot help.

**An agent's assessment of its own work is unreliable without executed evidence, and the quality of that evidence decides whether it helps.** On reasoning benchmarks with 2023 models, language models struggled to self-correct without external feedback and sometimes got worse. [Huang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.01798) Iterating on tests lifted GPT-4's pass@5 on competitive programming from 19% to 44%. [Ridnik et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.08500) Test quality determines the outcome: weak generated tests lowered an agent's resolve rate from 61.2% to 57.3% while strong tests raised it, and on the harder SWE-bench Pro generated tests added 0.7 points where oracle feedback added 11.8; the weak tests came from a separate test agent, so separation of authorship alone is not the cure. [Tao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.09133) Every frontier agent saturated a visible test suite while reward hacking persisted, and the gap grew by 28 percentage points for every tenfold increase in code size. [Zhao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.21384) An honest in-loop oracle redirected agents to build a demo that passed while the library it was meant to exercise was left dead or absent. [Ma et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.28430) Beck's notes from pairing with an agent record deleted assertions, deleted tests and faked implementation, and the observation that once complexity accumulates there is no path of tiny steps from here to there. [Beck 2025](https://newsletter.kentbeck.com/p/genie-wants-to-leap) Böckeler's exploratory evaluation of test-first inside the agent loop found no discernible difference in outcome quality and asked what purpose test-first retains without a human checkpoint between test and implementation. [Böckeler 2026](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/tdd-in-the-agent-loop.html)

**Under-specification is a first-class failure, and asking is cheap.** Models struggled to tell well-specified from under-specified instructions, yet interaction recovered up to 74% of the lost performance [Vijayvargiya et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.13069); a scaffold that detects uncertainty before executing reached a 69.4% resolve rate, closing most of the gap to fully specified tasks [Edwards and Schuster 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.26233); on under-specified operations tasks, 55.8–67.8% of runs violated at least one boundary because agents guessed rather than asked. [Ji et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.02294) A failure taxonomy from 1,642 multi-agent traces puts task-verification failures at 23.5% of all failures. [Cemri et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.13657)

**Reliable units are shorter than headline horizons.** METR's time-horizon work reports that the task length a model completes with 80% reliability is four to six times shorter than the 50% horizon that makes headlines, and its page notes that measurements above 16 hours are unreliable and that the horizon resembles what a low-context newcomer could do. [Kwa et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.14499); [METR time horizons page](https://metr.org/time-horizons/) Context is a finite resource that degrades with length. [Liu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.03172); [Hong et al. 2025](https://research.trychroma.com/context-rot) On a long-horizon benchmark, one frontier model's primary failure mode was context overflow (35.6%). [Deng et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.16941)

**First-party harness experience converges on the old practices, and then simplifies as models improve.** Anthropic's November 2025 long-running-agent experiment found that an agent tried to do too much at once and that later sessions declared the job done prematurely; the fixes were a feature list in JSON with every item initially failing, progress notes and git commits as the hand-off between context windows, the rule to "work on only one feature at a time", and end-to-end verification as a human user would do it. [Anthropic 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents) Its March 2026 follow-up removed the context resets and the sprint construct because a newer model could handle the work without that decomposition, kept the planner because without it the generator under-scoped, and kept a final evaluation pass; it also notes that every harness component encodes an assumption that can go stale as models improve. [Anthropic 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps) Its Claude Code guidance states the oracle principle plainly: without something to run that tells it whether the work is done, "looks done" is all the agent has. [Anthropic, Claude Code best practices](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-best-practices) OpenAI's team that shipped an internal product with no manually written code reports working depth-first from larger goals to smaller blocks, treating plans as first-class artifacts with execution plans, progress and decision logs checked into the repository, and replacing a single large instruction file, which "rots instantly", with a short map and pointers. [Lopopolo 2026](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/) Thoughtworks' April 2026 Radar names the corresponding practices and hazards: feedback sensors for coding agents (Trial), the Ralph loop of fresh-context iterations against a plan (Assess), and "codebase cognitive debt", the growing gap between a system's implementation and the team's understanding of it (Caution). [Thoughtworks Radar vol. 34](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)

**Harness gains shrink as models strengthen.** A 176-setting ablation found that a persistent planning scaffold moved from an accuracy aid for weaker models to a cost saver for stronger ones, with little change in accuracy. [Fan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20804) A contract-testing harness took a cheaper model from 3 of 10 successes to 10 of 10 but cost a frontier model about ten times the tokens for no gain. [Jain 2026](https://martinfowler.com/articles/are-harnesses-worth-the-cost.html) Mining of 9,374 trajectories found the model to be the primary driver of both outcome and behaviour. [Mehtiyev and Assunção 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.02547)

**Inference: what changed and what did not.** The mechanisms are the ones the 1970s papers described. The agent, like Royce's engineer, is optimistic about work it has not run; like Parnas and Clements' designers it cannot hold all the details; like Naur's replacement team it holds no theory between sessions. What changed are the prices. Producing code is now cheap and fast, so the bottleneck has moved to specifying intent, verifying results and keeping the shared understanding current. Review capacity and the discriminating power of the checks are the constraints that bind. A planning practice for agents therefore has to spend its effort on three things the old literature already named: grounding what each increment must produce in intent and domain evidence, keeping increments narrow enough for a reviewer and a context window, and carrying the theory in artifacts that are small, current and attributable because no agent will carry it in its head.

## The mechanisms, stated so that the boundaries follow

Six mechanisms account for the evidence above. Each has a condition under which it stops working, and the conditions are the boundaries of the recommendation.

1. **Feedback replaces forecast.** In the studied settings forecasts of value and duration were optimistic and did not improve with waiting; observed results do. Iteration works where an observation is cheap relative to the cost of being wrong, and stops working where observation is unavailable (no check that discriminates), unreliable (a check the implementer can satisfy without the deliverable, or one that measures a proxy) or late (a system that cannot be exercised until it is complete).

The agent evidence changes the weight of these mechanisms rather than their list. Mechanism 1 becomes dominant because the agent's forecast of its own success is the least reliable input in the system, so the checks must discriminate on the actual deliverable. Mechanism 2 becomes a constraint the plan must make visible, because the agent will not impose it on itself. Mechanism 6 becomes the hardest to satisfy, because the holder of the theory is now the plan plus a person, and the 2026 practitioner literature already has a name for what happens when that fails: codebase cognitive debt.

## Adaptation: from an understood objective to executable increments

This section is the study's proposal as frozen, revised after the cross-review only where the joint recommendation changed a condition; where the two differ, the joint recommendation governs. It is inference from the evidence above, not a tested design, and it assumes the existing pairing and review workflow: a pairing skill that advances by one observable check at a time, and a review process that gates landing. The planning practice proposed here produces the inputs those two consume. Each practice names its artifact and the check that tells whether it was done. Quantities below are defaults to be set in the pilot, not universal optima.

### 1. State the objective as an outcome with a signal

Write the objective in the hypothesis form: the capability, the outcome it should produce, and the observable signal that would show it did. [O'Reilly 2014](https://www.thoughtworks.com/insights/articles/how-implement-hypothesis-driven-development) Separate what is wanted in the world from what the system will do, and record the assumptions about the environment that the objective relies on; the earlier spec-driven studies in this directory cover that separation. Record the investment the operator is willing to make before any design is proposed, as Shape Up fixes an appetite before a design. [Singer 2019, ch. 3](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03)

*Artifact:* a concise objective statement with signal, assumptions and investment limit, with detail proportionate to the decision. *Check:* a reader who did not write it can say what would count as success and what would count as failure.

### 2. Inventory what is unknown and what is irreversible

List the questions whose answers would change the design (Armour's second-order ignorance made explicit), and the decisions that would be expensive to reverse: language and platform, layering, shared data stores, external interfaces and schemas, security and safety properties that must be designed in. [Poppendieck and Poppendieck 2003, ch. 3](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780321150783/samplepages/0321150783.pdf); [Leveson 2011](https://archive.org/download/mit_press_book_9780262298247/mit_press_book_9780262298247.pdf) Rank the unknowns by what it would cost to be wrong, following the risk-driven loop. [Fairbanks 2010](https://www.georgefairbanks.com/assets/jesa/Introduction.pdf) Decide the few consequential irreversibles breadth-first, with their owner and rationale recorded, reusing existing decision records, and leave genuinely deferred choices open with the condition that will trigger them written down. [Nygard 2011](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions); [Maassen and Matts 2007](https://www.infoq.com/articles/real-options-enhance-agility/) For a trivial task the list is empty and no record is required.

*Artifact:* a risk and decision list: each entry has a question or decision, its cost of being wrong, its owner, and either a decision record or a trigger condition. *Check:* every consequential irreversible decision has an owner and a recorded reason; every deferred decision has a trigger; no entry is a vague worry.

### 3. Spend spikes only on knowledge-limited questions

For each unknown whose answer can change a consequential decision and cannot be answered by reading, run a spike: one question, an investment bound set by the decision rather than a universal time quota, a disposal rule written before it starts, and an output that is a decision plus evidence, not code. [c2 wiki, SpikeSolution](http://xp.c2.com/SpikeSolution.html); [Wake 2000](https://xp123.com/how-do-you-do-a-performance-spike/) Prefer building the smallest real thing over spiking when the team already knows enough to build. [Lawrence and Green](https://www.humanizingwork.com/the-humanizing-work-guide-to-splitting-user-stories/) Agents make spikes cheap to run and expensive to trust: the spike's conclusion must come from an observation the operator can inspect, not from the agent's summary of it.

*Artifact:* a concise spike note, with detail proportionate to the decision: question, what was tried, what was observed, the decision, and the deletion or promotion of the code. *Check:* the code is gone or has been promoted through the normal path; the decision list entry now points at the note.

### 4. Inspect or establish the end-to-end path early

Inspect the real integration and verification path early; where it is consequentially unknown, make the first increment a thin end-to-end slice that links the main components and exercises deployment and verification, built with production habits and kept. [Cockburn, Walking skeleton](https://web.archive.org/web/2016id_/http://alistair.cockburn.us/Walking+skeleton); [Patton 2008](https://www.jpattonassociates.com/the-new-backlog/) In brownfield work the path usually exists and should be inspected rather than rebuilt; a model, domain inquiry or spike may come first when that is the cheaper way to resolve a decision-blocking risk. This is where integration surprises, the fat tail in the project data, surface at their cheapest. [Flyvbjerg et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.01573) For agent work it also establishes the harness: how to start the system, how to run the end-to-end check, and what a passing run looks like. [Anthropic 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)

*Artifact:* an inspected or newly built end-to-end path, its start instructions, and one end-to-end check that passes. *Check:* a fresh session can start the system and run the check from the written instructions alone.

### 5. Slice the remainder into increments, each with grounded expected behaviour, discriminating checks and a declared reach

Split the objective into vertical slices using the INVEST criteria and the ordinary splitting patterns. [Wake 2003](https://xp123.com/articles/invest-in-good-stories-and-smart-tasks/) For each increment, ground its expected behaviour in governing intent and domain evidence (an accepted requirement or operator decision, a domain rule, an existing contract, a defect report or reproducer, or an explicitly authorised design choice) and record that source. State the checks that would discriminate a relevant wrong result from the intended one on the actual deliverable, and what those checks do not establish; this is the observable condition the pairing skill consumes as its first check, and the pair writes the tests from that settled intent. Because one agent's test and code can share an error and because weak tests lower outcomes, use independent review, consumer acceptance, held-out examples, differential checks, models or user observation where risk or ambiguity warrants them: externally visible behaviour, data that cannot be restored, or a slice whose visible tests could be satisfied by a demo rather than the deliverable. [Tao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.09133); [Ma et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.28430); [Jain 2026](https://martinfowler.com/articles/are-harnesses-worth-the-cost.html) Existing review requirements are preserved; agent agreement and a passing self-consistent test suite alone do not establish whole-objective success.

Declare each increment's expected reach, the modules and subsystems it will touch, alongside its size, because in the studied systems breadth predicted failure at least as well as line count; treat exceeding the declared reach as a risk signal to report with a reason, not as a hard cap that fragments a coherent behaviour. [Mockus and Weiss 2000](https://mockus.org/papers/bltj13.pdf); [Kamei et al. 2013](https://posl.ait.kyushu-u.ac.jp/~kamei/publications/Kamei_TSE2013.pdf) Size an increment that an agent will run unattended well below the model's headline capability, in line with the gap between 50% and 80% time horizons. [Kwa et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.14499) Choose order by consequential uncertainty, value and learning cost together; when replanning shows changes getting harder or understanding diverging from the implementation, add a bounded consolidation increment with a stated purpose and evidence, while ordinary refactoring stays inside the pair's loop. [Foote and Yoder 1999](http://www.laputan.org/mud/mud.html)

*Artifact:* an increment list in a machine-stable format in which each entry carries the source of its expected behaviour, its checks, its declared reach, its status, and the question it will answer if any. *Check:* every entry's checks can be run or inspected by someone other than its author; a reach exceeded is reported with a reason.

### 6. Plan in rolling waves and record plan changes with their reasons

Prepare the next increment precisely, and more only where a real dependency benefits; keep the rest at the level of the question each will answer. After each increment, revisit the risk list, the decision list and the remaining slices in the light of what was learned, and record what changed and why. [Boehm 1988](https://faculty.washington.edu/hazeline/misc/reserve/boehm_spiralmodel.pdf) Keep the plan small enough to be read in one sitting; the evidence from agent harnesses is that large instruction documents rot while a short map plus pointers survives. [Lopopolo 2026](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/) Agents may refine implementation detail, split or reorder work, update evidence and revise the current plan within the accepted objective, constraints, architecture and investment; consequential deltas and their reasons are recorded. Changes to product policy, commitments, required outcomes, accepted risks or authority go to their owner unless already delegated, and an obligation is never weakened merely to make a check pass: a correction to a flawed check stays traceable to the governing requirement and receives the applicable review. [Anthropic 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents); [Beck 2025](https://newsletter.kentbeck.com/p/genie-wants-to-leap)

*Artifact:* the increment list and decision list under version control, with a short log of plan changes. *Check:* the plan's current state can be reconstructed from the log; no obligation was weakened without a recorded, reviewed reason; recent learning entries point at the increments that produced them.

### 7. Integrate and verify every increment end to end

Each increment lands through the normal review path, small enough for a reviewer to hold in mind, and its completion is distinguished from the pair's candidate verification: an increment is complete when its discriminating checks have been observed on the agreed integration boundary, with integration reported as pending where landing lies outside the current authority. [Sadowski et al. 2018](https://sback.it/publications/icse2018seip.pdf); [Anthropic 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents) Verified code and validated outcome remain different claims; an unobserved outcome stays pending even when code is correct. The operator's decisions sit at commitments and outcomes, not at every diff: the decision list, changes to required outcomes, and the objective's signal.

*Artifact:* an observed passing run on the integration boundary and a landed or integration-pending change per increment. *Check:* the increment's checks pass on the integrated code, not only on the branch; the signal from practice 1 is re-examined when the last increment lands.

### 8. Keep the theory current

Because no agent carries the system's theory between sessions, and because a team's understanding of a fast-changing codebase drifts, treat the artifacts above as the shared theory and keep them small, current and attributed: the objective statement, the decision records, the increment list, the glossary of designated terms, and the progress log. [Naur 1985](https://pablo.rauzy.name/dev/naur1985programming.pdf); [Thoughtworks Radar vol. 34, Codebase cognitive debt](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/codebase-cognitive-debt) A person remains the holder of last resort; the plan's job is to make that person's intervention cheap by giving them the decision and its evidence, not the transcript.

### Ceremony by risk

Not every objective needs every artifact. A change confined to one module with an existing test suite needs the objective, its checks and the landing, and can pass through the practice in minutes; a change that touches a shared schema, an external interface or a safety property needs the decision records and an inspected end-to-end path; a change that nobody can estimate needs a spike before it needs a plan. The selector is the cost of being wrong and the reversibility of the decision, consistent with the ceremony-by-risk principle the earlier spec-driven studies adopted. The measure of whether the practice is working is the one Basili and Turner proposed in 1975: modifications should get easier as increments progress, and if they get harder the design, not the plan, is the problem. [Basili and Turner 1975, p. 391](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~basili/publications/journals/J04.pdf)

## What remains unknown and how to refresh this study

**Unknowns that could change the recommendation.**

- No reliable controlled estimate of productivity or quality for the current paired-agent workflow on realistic brownfield work was located in the sources reviewed; METR's 2025 result stands for its tools and setting, and METR withdrew its follow-up design without a replacement result. A clean result in either direction would change how much of the plan's effort should go to verification.
- No causal evidence links plan or specification quality to agent outcomes; the one large study is null for specification artifacts in pull-request workflows, and the practitioner evidence is qualitative. A controlled comparison of increment-level planning against full up-front specification would settle the main choice in this study.
- The harness-ablation trend, in which planning scaffolds move from accuracy gains to cost savings as models strengthen, might extend to reach expectations and evaluation independence. The sources read do not test that, and the reward-hacking results argue against assuming it.
- Review capacity under high volumes of small agent-authored changes is unmeasured in the opened literature. If it degrades faster than expected, the plan's increment count must be capped harder than this study proposes.
- Whether grounding expected behaviour and discriminating checks per increment is affordable at the granularity proposed here is untested in this repository; the pilot measures in the joint recommendation are designed to answer it.

**Coverage limits.** The agile outcome literature was read through its systematic review, two Norwegian field studies, a 1999 Beck article and publisher samples; Beck's books, the pair-programming meta-analysis and two TDD reviews were not opened. Flyvbjerg's book and Snowden and Boone's article were read through the authors' papers; Boehm's 1984 prototyping experiment was read through a later review. DORA's reported 2026 survey pause is known only from secondary sources and is unconfirmed. The agent literature was read as of late October 2026 and is moving quickly.

**Refresh protocol.** Re-check, in this order: METR's productivity and time-horizon pages; DORA's research index and any 2026 publication; the ESEM, ICSE, FSE and ASE proceedings for replications of Hill 2026 and for controlled studies of plan scaffolds, evaluation independence and clarification scaffolds; Anthropic's and OpenAI's engineering posts on harnesses; the Thoughtworks Radar techniques quadrant at each edition; and the repository's own pilot measures once the practice has been tried on two or three objectives. ## Conclusion

The hypothesis is right that the rules are old. Royce, Basili and Turner, Parnas, Brooks, Boehm, Gilb and Naur wrote down, between 1970 and 1988, the reasons iteration helps and the conditions under which it fails, and the 2025–2026 agent literature has rediscovered both. The hypothesis is unsupported wherever it treats delivery frequency, spikes or co-evolution as proven causes of success: the outcome evidence is mixed and mostly observational, null findings do not prove absence of benefit, and the practitioners who invented the practices documented their failure modes. What the evidence supports is narrower and more useful. In the studied settings forecasts erred toward optimism, so test rather than predict. Breadth of change predicted failure there at least as well as size, so declare reach. Review and context are finite, so keep increments small enough for a reader. Integration drives the fat tail, so inspect or establish the end-to-end path early. A few decisions are irreversible, so decide those early and record why. Growth without consolidation decays, so consolidate when evidence shows it. The theory of the system lives in people and no agent keeps it between sessions, so carry it in small current artifacts and keep a person as its holder. The adaptation section turns those into a plan-building practice with an artifact and a check for each step, sized to the existing pairing and review workflow; the joint recommendation states the agreed direction and conditions, and the frozen position says what would change the recommendation and how a pilot could.
